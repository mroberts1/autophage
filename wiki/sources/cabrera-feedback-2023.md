# Feedback — Cabrera (2023)

A chapter in *Sound Affects: A User's Guide* (Bloomsbury Academic, 2023), ed. Mee and Robinson. Full PDF at `raw/papers/Mandel Cabrera - Feedback.pdf`.

## Central Argument

Acoustic feedback defines a "zone of precarity": a situation in which the smallest movements can trigger an escalating reinjection cycle, making the sonic environment something we are vulnerable to rather than in control of. The zone cannot be mastered — only inhabited. Cabrera maps two contrasting postures artists have taken toward it: conquest (taming the cycle) and non-mastery (embracing affect, yielding to the loop). The chapter argues the second posture is philosophically richer and organises its case around three figures: Robert Ashley, Alvin Lucier, and Eliane Radigue.

## The Zone of Precarity

The material principle of feedback is the escalating cycle: sound is received, transduced to electronic signals, amplified, transduced back to sound, and run back through the chain again. Each pass through the loop intensifies the signal. The zone is precarious because its contours — which movements risk uncontrollable escalation, which bring safety — are nuanced, dynamic, and difficult to discern in real time.

The standard response (in performance and in everyday sound work) is withdrawal: swing the mic away, lower the gain, reassert mastery. But some artists have instead moved toward the zone, using feedback as medium rather than mistake.

## Three Artists

**Robert Ashley — *The Wolfman* (1964)**
A performer sings softly into a mic fed into an amplifier cranked so high that any sound triggers feedback. The performer's mouth shape modulates the feedback, creating the impression of feedback swirling around the audience. Ashley cast the vocalist as aggressor — a "sinister nightclub vocalist" — staging implicit violence between performer and audience. The Wolfman set the stage for works that would situate both as equally subject to the escalating cycle's vagaries rather than one doing the subjecting.

**Steve Reich — *Pendulum Music* (1968)**
Several mics are suspended on cables above speakers lying on the floor, each connected to an amplifier at feedback threshold. Performers pull back the mics and release them; as they swing freely over the speakers, rhythmic feedback pulses emerge, shifting according to the entropic slowing of the swings, terminating in static hum when the mics come to rest above the speakers. Reich instructed performers to "sit down and watch like the rest of the audience." *Pendulum Music* stages the erasure of the boundary between performer and audience; both are witnesses rather than manipulators of the escalating cycle. Described by Reich as "the ultimate process piece... audible sculpture."

**Alvin Lucier — multiple works (1965–1975)**
Lucier's relationship with feedback is the most sustained and methodologically varied in the chapter. Key works:

- *Music for Solo Performer* (1965): electrodes pick up the performer's alpha brain waves, fed into loudspeakers and percussion instruments, whose sounds trigger further brain waves — a biofeedback loop in which the performer becomes "simply another element in the system."
- *Shelter* (1967): contact mics on walls, doorframes, and doors of a hall feed environmental sounds into the space, making the building resonate with its own acoustic signature.
- *I Am Sitting in a Room* (1969): a spoken text is recorded, played back into the room, re-recorded, and repeated until the resonant frequencies of the room overwhelm the speech, destroying its semantic content. The room speaks its own acoustic properties through the gradual disintegration of language. Cabrera reads this as the artist's erasure through the very material by which they express themselves — destruction and articulation bound into one.
- *Bird and Person Dyning* (1975): a performer wanders a space with binaural mics, picking up a tiny birdcall (a Christmas ornament) via loudspeakers, creating complex spatialized heterodyning. The body itself enters the zone of precarity.

**Eliane Radigue — feedback works (1967–1970)**
The chapter's climax. Working in isolation after leaving Pierre Henry's Studio Apsome (where her feedback work was dismissed), Radigue produced a series of acousmatic pieces built entirely from acoustic and tape feedback: *Jouet Electronique* (1967), *Σ = a = b* (1969), *Usal* (1969), *Omnht* (1970), *Vice-Versa, etc...* (1970), *Stress-Osaka* (1970), *Opus 17* (1970).

Radigue's framing concept, drawn from her essay "The Mysterious Power of the Infinitesimal" (*Leonardo Music Journal*, 2009): feedback discloses what is normally inaudible — frequencies implicit in mechanical parts, sounds too quiet for human hearing — by amplifying them into audibility via the escalating cycle. Feedback is thus access to "nature's feral riches," the "immense vibrating symphony of the universe."

Her method was near-stillness: endless hours holding a microphone, shifting it by infinitesimal increments, letting her ears and hands be choreographed by the most fragile of sounds. Where Ashley conquered and Reich observed, Radigue *apprenticed herself* to the zone — not to exploit it, but to dwell in it in equanimity. The zone of precarity becomes, for her, not a site of danger but the all-encompassing world itself: the "ever-present rhythm of the breath, pulsations, beating" that constitutes our intimacy with the cosmic.

## Key Quotations

> "Acoustic feedback emerges within a zone of precarity: a situation in which the possibilities for sounding and listening, for act and affect, become fraught with Damoclean dangers."

> "In feedback, we hear silence with the volume turned way up: aspects of the vibratory continuum that are normally undetectable by our ears — sounds too quiet to be heard, frequencies implicit in mechanical parts — magnified to audibility by the escalating cycle."

> "For Radigue, feedback is simply one form in which this language of 'breath, pulsations, and beating' speaks through us, yet therefore exceeds us."
