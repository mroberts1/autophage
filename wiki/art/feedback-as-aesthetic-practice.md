# Feedback as Aesthetic Practice

Feedback is a condition, not a technique. It occurs whenever a system's output is routed back into its own input — a loop that either stabilizes or amplifies, converges or explodes. As an aesthetic practice, feedback means making that loop visible, audible, or experiential: turning the self-referential structure of a system into the work itself.

The history of feedback aesthetics is largely a history of artists discovering what happens when you point a camera at its own monitor, hold a guitar pickup to its own speaker, or wire a microphone into its own amplifier — and then staying with what happens next rather than cutting the loop.

## Video feedback

The foundational gesture in video art is also its most literal feedback act: the camera pointed at the monitor displaying its own image. Nam June Paik's earliest video experiments in the 1960s circled this possibility. Peter Campus, working at MIT in the early 1970s, developed *Interface* (1972) and related closed-circuit installations in which the viewer's image arrived onscreen delayed, doubled, or phase-shifted — feedback used to estrange the self from its own reflection.

Steina and Woody Vasulka built custom analog video synthesizers at The Kitchen in New York in the 1970s that used feedback as a primary compositional material. Their *Noisefields* and related works treated the feedback loop as a generative system: the image folds, tears, and reconstitutes itself according to the physics of the electronic signal, not the logic of representation. Feedback here is not a glitch to be corrected but a material property of the medium to be shaped.

The physical behavior of video feedback — recursive nesting, spiral zooms, color cycling, interference patterns — prefigures the mathematical structures of fractal geometry. Benoit Mandelbrot formalized the self-similar structures that appear in iterated function systems at almost exactly the moment video artists were discovering them empirically with cameras and monitors. The connection is not merely visual: both involve iterating a function on its own output and observing the attractor.

## Acoustic feedback

In music, feedback entered the aesthetic vocabulary through amplified electric instruments. Jimi Hendrix, the Velvet Underground, and Free Jazz musicians like Albert Ayler all treated feedback tones as expressive material rather than noise to suppress.

Manuel Cabrera's chapter "Feedback" in *Sound Affects* (Bloomsbury, 2023) — [[cabrera-feedback-2023]] — organises the acoustic feedback tradition around a central distinction: artists who sought to *conquer* the escalating cycle versus those who embraced *non-mastery*, yielding to the loop rather than controlling it. His three key figures are Robert Ashley, Alvin Lucier, and Eliane Radigue.

**Robert Ashley — *The Wolfman* (1964)** staged feedback as weapon: the performer's mouth shape modulated feedback swirling around the audience, casting the vocalist as predator. Ashley set the terms for what would follow by making both performer and audience subject to the loop.

**Steve Reich — *Pendulum Music* (1968)** removed the performer entirely: mics swing freely over speakers at feedback threshold, generating rhythmic pulses that slow entropically until all that remains is static hum. Reich described it as "the ultimate process piece... audible sculpture." Both performers and audience sit down and watch.

**Alvin Lucier** built the most sustained body of feedback work: *Music for Solo Performer* (1965) used biofeedback from brain waves; *Shelter* (1967) turned a building into its own resonator via contact mics on walls; *I Am Sitting in a Room* (1969) recorded speech into a room until the room's resonant frequencies consumed the language entirely, leaving only acoustic signature. The content is destroyed by iteration; what survives is structure. This is model collapse, avant la lettre. In *Bird and Person Dyning* (1975), Lucier himself entered the feedback zone with binaural mics, becoming "simply another element in the system."

**Eliane Radigue** is the chapter's culminating figure. Working in near-isolation after leaving Pierre Henry's studio, she produced acousmatic pieces built entirely from tape and acoustic feedback — *Σ = a = b* (1969), *Usal*, *Omnht*, *Stress-Osaka* (all 1970) — through a method of near-stillness: holding a microphone for hours, shifting it by infinitesimal increments, letting her hands be choreographed by the most fragile of sounds. Her frame for this, from her essay "The Mysterious Power of the Infinitesimal": feedback discloses what is normally inaudible — frequencies too quiet for the ear, implicit in the vibration of objects — by amplifying them via the escalating cycle. Feedback is access to "nature's feral riches," the "immense vibrating symphony of the universe."

## Feedback and cybernetics

The theoretical frame for all of this is Norbert Wiener's cybernetics, formalized in *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine* (1948). Wiener defined feedback as the mechanism by which a system corrects its own behavior by measuring the gap between its current state and a target state. Negative feedback reduces the gap (thermostat, homeostasis). Positive feedback amplifies it (microphone howl, arms races, viral spread).

For artists working in the 1960s–70s, cybernetics offered something that modernist aesthetics had not: a model of the artwork as a system rather than an object, one whose behavior could not be predicted from its components alone but only observed as it ran. Roy Ascott's *Behaviourist Art and the Cybernetic Vision* (1966–67) remains the clearest articulation of this program.

## Recursive media as feedback

The broader concept of [[recursive-media]] extends feedback from a technical condition to a formal category: any medium that takes itself as its own content, tool, or material. A film about filmmaking, a novel in which the narrator writes the novel we are reading, a mirror facing a mirror — all are feedback structures operating at the level of representation rather than signal.

What connects video feedback, Lucier's room recordings, and contemporary concerns about [[model-collapse]] is precisely this formal structure: a loop in which each iteration takes the output of the last as its input, and in which something is lost, amplified, or transformed with each pass. The question feedback art asks — what survives the loop, and what does the loop do to what survives — is the same question being posed now about generative AI trained on its own output.
